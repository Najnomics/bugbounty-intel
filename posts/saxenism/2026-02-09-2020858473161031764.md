> This is a take I strongly align with, because it clearly identifies why security is such a big deal in Web3.
> 
> You cannot afford to "experiment" with people's hard-earned money. Period.
> 
> ---
> 
> That said, the downstream impact on smaller teams is real, and it shouldn't be brushed under the carpet. Smaller players do have to fight a BRUTAL, UPHILL battle. 
> 
> If we actually want to fix this, I'd request @abbaskhan to direct attention and resources towards the following three concrete levers:
> 
> 1. Make monitoring a first-class citizen of web3 security: 
> Today, the "security" of a web3 protocol starts and ends at smart contract audits. Meanwhile, ~60% of major compromises trace back to *non-contract* attack vectors like DNS hijacking, supply chain attacks, web2 infrastructure, phishing, and social engineering.
> 
> As a fail-safe against the many attack vectors that exist and the limited security budget of protocols, robust, strict monitoring that can trigger automated on-chain protections is a must.
> 
> 2. Solve on-chain insurance of user funds:
> This one is simple.
> 
> If I am a DeFi user and I can be assured that if my funds get stolen, I will be refunded my full amount... I will use the protocol even if all other security guarantees are weak.
> 
> 3. Normalise gradual open-sourcing of source code:
> I understand the values and ethos behind demanding everything to be open-sourced from day one... but there is merit in the "security-in-obscurity" methodology. 
> Even more so for smaller teams with limited time and resources dedicated to security.
> 
> ----
> 
> Security expectations in crypto are NOT irrational.
> But failures remain expensive, and to give smaller teams a fair shot at it, we have to focus on the above three levers.

> Some audit bros are on my ass, so here’s the full explanation:
> 
> In other areas of tech, experimentation is very easy and cheap. You can launch projects, test demand, see what sticks, and shut down what doesn't. The cost of learning whether an idea is worth pursuing is very low. 
> 
> This encourages speed, iteration, and risk-taking. Most projects fail, and that’s fine, because failure is affordable when it comes at a low cost.
> 
> In crypto, experimentation is expensive from day one. 
> 
> The moment a product touches user funds, expectations jump straight to production grade security. No one wants to interact with a codebase that hasn't been audited, often multiple times. This is rational behavior because the stakes are high.
> 
> As a result, crypto ends up biased towards teams that already have capital. Smaller and bootstrapped teams don't launch at all or they take shortcuts that increase risk, neither of which is healthy.
>  
> This raises the barrier to entry and filters out a huge amount of projects. Many ideas never get tested and we lose out on useful potential projects.
> 
> If crypto wants more innovation, it needs to make it cheaper to fail early.
> 
> Right now, failure is too expensive, and that’s one of the biggest reasons experimentation in crypto lags behind other major technology sectors.
