> The ironic thing is that this will sound to many like old people come across when irrationally fearing technology. But this time the "technophobes" are right, and it's just a matter of how many AI catastrophes need to happen until the rest understand it.
>
> Here's the core truth accelerationists don't want people to understand: every non-AI process running on your computer can, with some amount of effort, be explained by a computer scientist. Once AI is involved, the "why" something works is gone, and all you're left with is a pattern of processing that seems right on the surface. But without understanding the piping, by definition you cannot make any positive statement about its correctness.
>
> Any time humans or computers act based on LLM output it carries an unbounded leak risk. To be clear this is not going away in the next Claude or ChatGPT release, it's completely structural and baked into the tech. Every user needs to decide whether the risk/reward tradeoff is worth it. The problem starts when (a) users are kept blind about these risks and (b) powerful players make the opt-in decision for everyone.
>
> That is not to say that LLMs are not incredibly useful. Unfortunately (not particularly smart) people keep equating the arguments "LLMs are unreliable" and "LLMs are useless", this way they can cancel you and lets them keep chasing unrealistic world views. 
>
> I seriously hope I'm wrong about this, but we're going to see more and more "rm -rf /" tier screw ups in the coming years. Anyone reading tech news is already seeing this acceleration. But what's even more worrying than the computational leak is the human processing leak. What happens when elections are decided based on wrong, or manipulated premises? Can't say I'm waiting to found out. Humanity had an arguably good thing going before this, it's too bad financial greed (stock has to go up right?) is going to send us crashing into walls without as much as a seat belt.
