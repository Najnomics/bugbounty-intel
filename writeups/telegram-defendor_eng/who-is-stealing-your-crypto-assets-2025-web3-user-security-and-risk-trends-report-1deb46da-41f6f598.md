### Who Is Stealing Your Crypto Assets? — 2025 Web3 User Security and Risk Trends Report

### Chapter 1: A Grim Security Landscape in 2025

In 2025, according to data from the **GoPlus RektDatabase**, the Web3 space experienced over **1,200** major security incidents involving users and project teams, resulting in total losses exceeding **USD 3.5 billion**.

The overall security landscape shows two clear trends: an **increase in the number of ultra-large-scale incidents** and a **significant reduction in the cost of conducting small-scale fraud against users**. This indicates that attackers are simultaneously adopting strategies of **“precision targeting”** and **“wide-net exploitation.”**

### 1.1 Top Loss Incidents of 2025 (Precision Targeting Attacks)

- **February 21** — **Bybit** was attacked, resulting in losses of approximately **USD 1.5 billion**. The cause was the compromise of a Safe multisig server, where transaction instructions were tampered with. **Follow-up actions:** Bybit cooperated with law enforcement agencies including the FBI, freezing over **USD 40 million** in stolen assets. The platform replenished reserves to ensure that user funds were not affected.

The price of **$CETUS** dropped by more than **16%**. **Follow-up actions:** The Sui network quickly coordinated to freeze **USD 162 million** of the stolen assets. A community vote approved a **100% compensation plan**, with additional **USD 30 million** in emergency credit support from the Sui Foundation.
- **November 3** — **Balancer** was attacked, with multi-chain losses of approximately **USD 128 million**, due to a vulnerability in the Balancer V2 smart contract. **Follow-up actions:** Berachain coordinated validators to execute an emergency hard fork and freeze the attacker’s addresses. White-hat operators returned **USD 12.8 million**, and StakeWise recovered approximately **USD 20 million** using contract management mechanisms.
- **June 18** — **Iranian exchange Nobitex** was attacked, resulting in multi-chain losses of approximately **USD 90 million**. **Follow-up actions:** This incident had clear geopolitical overtones. - **January 23** — **Phemex** was attacked, suffering multi-chain losses of approximately **USD 69.1 million**, due to a compromised hot wallet. **Follow-up actions:** The platform released Proof of Reserves (PoR) to confirm cold wallet security and compensated affected users in full through phased reimbursements using its own funds.
- **August 14** — **Turkish exchange BtcTurk** was attacked, with losses of approximately **USD 54 million**, caused by a hot wallet breach. - **December 2** — **South Korean exchange Upbit** was attacked, suffering losses of approximately **USD 30 million** on **Solana**, attributed to infiltration by a **North Korean hacking group**. **Follow-up actions:** Upbit cooperated with global exchanges to implement real-time blacklist tagging and tracking/interception of funds linked to the attackers.

### 1.2 High-Frequency Attacks and Fraud Types (Wide-Net Exploitation)

According to data from the **GoPlus RektDatabase**, among Web3 security incidents in 2025, the three most frequent attack and fraud types were **private key theft** (via malware and social engineering), **phishing attacks**, and **rug tokens** (fraudulent tokens).

### Private Key Theft

In 2025, private key theft fully shifted from early-stage “broad, indiscriminate attacks” to **highly targeted hunting**. Attackers increasingly focused on **individual accounts with multisig permissions** or **centralized service providers**.

- **Number of incidents:** Over **300 cases** throughout the year.
- **Total losses:** Exceeded **USD 1.8 billion** (accounting for **more than 50%** of total annual losses).
- **Key characteristics:**

1. **Malware-based attacks:** Attackers disguised malicious software as popular Web3 productivity tools, node maintenance scripts, or DeFi analytics plugins, seducing users to download them and implanting **private key stealers**.
2. **Social engineering:** A typical example is the **“fake Zoom” social engineering attack**. Attackers impersonated recruiters or project teams, invited core developers to interviews, and then delivered malicious “coding test tasks” or “meeting plugins” containing backdoors to gain device control and steal private keys.
3. **Supply chain compromise:** Targets shifted from **wallet files** to **development environments**. In 2025, incidents caused by compromised development setups or the installation of malicious third-party dependencies leading to private key leakage increased significantly.

### Phishing Attacks

Phishing attacks remained the **most frequent** threat type in 2025, and with the widespread adoption of AI technologies, the number of fraud cases grew rapidly.

- **Number of incidents:** Over **400 cases** throughout the year (large-scale, recorded incidents; small retail phishing cases are difficult to quantify).
- **Total losses:** Exceeded **USD 750 million**.
- **Trend analysis:**

1. **AI-generated phishing:** Attackers used large language models to automatically generate multilingual phishing emails and posts that were highly tailored to the preferences and behaviors of target users.
2. **Cross-platform expansion:** Phishing vectors expanded from **X (Twitter)** to **Telegram**, **Discord**, and even **LinkedIn**.
3. **Signature traps:** In 2025, **signature-based phishing** remained the dominant method, with attackers seduction users to sign malicious approvals and subsequently draining their assets.
4. **Defense upgrades:** Real-time detection by security extensions and wallet-side **transaction preview** features significantly reduced the success rate of low-level phishing, pushing attackers toward more advanced and interactive fraud techniques.

### Rug Tokens (Malicious Liquidity Pulls / Exit Scams)

In 2025, rug tokens showed a clear shift from **frequent small-scale scams** to **single, large-scale exits**, especially as the boom in the meme market gave rise to more sophisticated extraction techniques.

- **Number of incidents:** Approximately **300 cases** throughout the year (with high concentration on **Base**, **BSC**, and **Solana**).
- **Total losses:** Exceeded **USD 600 million** (excluding certain disputed price collapses).
- **Key characteristics:**

1. **Fragmented rug pulls:** Developers no longer remove all liquidity at once. Instead, they use large numbers of related addresses (**puppet wallets**) to withdraw small amounts over several days, avoiding monitoring alerts.
2. **Hidden backdoors:** Malicious teams hide backdoors via **proxy contracts**. After passing initial audits, they exploit upgrade mechanisms to modify contract logic, insert malicious functions, or grant super-admin privileges — executing a “technical exit.”
3. **Professionalization and syndication:** An underground industry offering **“Rug Pull as a Service”** has emerged, providing end-to-end solutions including code development, fake marketing, and volume manipulation.
4. **Meme coins as the main battlefield:** Over **90%** of rug tokens occurred in the meme coin sector, which lacks real business fundamentals.

### Chapter 2: Typical Cases and Attack Technique Breakdown

### 2.1 Bybit — An Epic Multisig Frontend Tampering Attack

This was the **most devastating security incident in the global Web3 space in 2025**, a textbook **supply-chain attack**. Instead of attacking the target directly, the attackers compromised the multisig infrastructure it relied on — **Safe{Wallet}**.

- **Technical Breakdown & Attack Flow**

1. **Frontend compromise:** The North Korean hacking group **Lazarus Group** compromised the device of a Safe{Wallet} developer who had system release permissions. They then gained access to Safe’s AWS account and injected malicious scripts into the official frontend code.
2. **Targeted fraud:** The malicious script was activated **only for Bybit’s specific multisig wallet addresses**. When Bybit’s three multisig administrators used the compromised official Safe frontend for routine fund transfers, the interface appeared normal, but the underlying transaction data was secretly replaced with calls to a malicious contract pre-deployed by the attackers.
3. **Blind signing vulnerability:** When administrators signed transactions using hardware wallets, the devices were unable to fully parse and display the complex contract call data, resulting in **“blind signing”** and failure to detect the abnormal transaction.
4. **Control takeover:** Through a DelegateCall, the malicious transaction upgraded the wallet’s logic contract to one controlled by the attacker, granting full control over the multisig wallet and enabling the transfer of massive amounts of assets.

- **Security Recommendations**

1. **For project teams:** Operations involving large sums of assets should implement **independent, multi-party verification mechanisms**, avoiding reliance on a single frontend or service for all signers.
2. **For users:** When signing critical transactions, always carefully verify the **transaction hash and detailed call data** on wallet devices, and remain highly cautious of any **blind signing** requests.

### 2.2 UXLINK — AI Deepfake & Social Engineering Attack

This case shows that **humans remain the weakest security link**, even with multisig.

- **Analysis & Process:**

1. **AI Deepfake Trust:** Attack began before Sept 22; attackers impersonated trusted partners on Telegram, using video calls with real-time face and voice cloning to gain trust.
2. **Trojan Implant:** During calls, personnel clicked malicious links, implanting malware, leaking multiple multisig keys.
3. **Contract Takeover:** Attackers removed original admins and added themselves using stolen keys.
4. **Theft & Minting:** Existing wallet assets were stolen; attackers then maliciously minted UXLINK tokens and sold on market.

- **Security Recommendations:**

1. **Permission Isolation:** Separate **fund wallets** from **permission wallets**; avoid holding significant funds in permission wallets and physically isolate signing devices.
2. **Personnel Security:** Continuous security awareness training; vigilance against social engineering.
3. **Governance Upgrades:** Update and replace deployed contracts and admin addresses to mitigate risks.

### 2.3 Balancer — Computational Precision & Smart Contract Vulnerability

This incident demonstrates how **tiny errors in complex business logic and mathematical calculations** in DeFi protocols can trigger massive risks and losses.

- **Analysis & Process:**

1. **Root Cause:** The Balancer attack stemmed from a **precision issue (rounding down)**.
2. During swap calculations in the Balancer Vault, results were rounded down, affecting token prices inside the Vault.
3. batchSwap amplified this vulnerability: by carefully crafting batch swap parameters, attackers could significantly depress Vault token prices and profit from the resulting price difference.

- **Security Recommendations:**

1. **Continuous Deep Business Logic Audits:** Use more advanced and efficient security techniques such as AI and formal verification to improve vulnerability discovery, especially for deep business logic issues that cannot rely solely on manual audits.

### Chapter 3: Evolution of Attack Methods and Emerging Tech Scams in 2025

In 2025, the evolution of Web3 security threats showed **significant technological convergence, intelligence, and automation**. Attackers no longer rely on a single technique but deeply integrate new protocol features, supply chain vulnerabilities, AI, and automated tools to create attacks that are **more destructive, stealthy, and cost-effective**.

### 3.1 EIP-7702 and New Phishing Attacks

EIP-7702, a core proposal of Ethereum’s Pectra upgrade, aims to **enable smart EOAs**, enhancing account abstraction and user experience. However, this innovation was quickly weaponized by hackers, spawning phishing attacks with **lower barriers to entry and higher stealth**.

**Mechanism:**

The core mechanism of EIP-7702 allows EOAs to temporarily inject code via the SetCode operation to enable **delegated authorization**. Attackers exploit this by pre-controlling leaked private key addresses and authorizing them to malicious Delegator contracts, which automatically transfer any funds the user later deposits.

Unlike traditional phishing, this model **does not rely on frontend UI fraud**, and conventional malicious authorization or signature detection mechanisms fail, further amplifying risk. Users fall prey to the “private key equals assets” misconception, leading to losses. Combined with **batch execution (Batch)**, the efficiency of phishing attacks is significantly increased.

**EIP-7702 New Phishing Typically Follows Four Stages:**

1. **Preparation:** Attackers obtain private keys from the dark web or historical leaks and authorize them to malicious Delegator contracts deployed across multiple chains (commonly ETH, BSC, Base).
2. **Inducement:** Attackers use DMs or social media to expose users to these private keys, making them believe it’s a “free gain.”
3. **Execution:** Once the user deposits Gas or tokens, the malicious Delegator contract automatically withdraws the funds, causing immediate loss.
4. **Laundering:** Funds are automatically transferred via mixers or cross-chain bridges, making tracking difficult.

### 2. Software Supply Chain Attacks: Self-Propagating Worms & Cross-Ecosystem Damage

In 2025, open-source supply chain attacks surged. Worm-like malware such as **Shai-Hulud** and its 2.0 variant caused a **global impact** on ecosystems like npm and Maven, affecting over **25,000 GitHub repositories**.

**Mechanism:**

Unlike single-package poisoning, Shai-Hulud uses a **self-replicating worm mechanism**: after compromising a maintainer’s account, it injects postinstall/preinstall scripts to steal credentials and propagate automatically. It leverages **TruffleHog** to scan the file system and steal AWS/GCP/Azure keys, npm tokens, and GitHub PATs. The worm then traverses the dependency graph, injects malicious payloads, publishes new versions, and spreads exponentially.

**Software supply chain attacks generally follow three stages:**

1. **Initial Compromise:** Attackers steal developer account credentials for popular open-source projects (e.g., debug, chalk) via phishing or other methods and inject malicious code into repositories.
2. **Worm Behavior:** Malicious scripts (e.g., setup\_bun.js) execute during installation (preinstall), automatically stealing npm tokens and GitHub PATs from the developer’s environment. Using these credentials, the worm scans all other repositories owned by the developer, injects malicious code, and republishes, achieving cross-project infection.
3. **Execution & Theft:** Once other developers use the infected repositories, attackers perform remote loading and background execution to steal funds or cause damage.
4. **Cross-Ecosystem Propagation:** The infection spreads from npm to Maven/Java, with attack windows ranging from hours to days, affecting **over 1 billion downloads**.

**Further Cases:**

Shai-Hulud 2.0 impacted over **700 npm packages** and **25,000 GitHub repositories**. Given that underlying libraries were downloaded hundreds of millions of times per week, thousands of DApp frontends unknowingly became vectors for fund theft globally.

The threat of software supply chain attacks is not limited to mass propagation; **targeted attacks on individual projects** also require vigilance. On **December 25**, Trust Wallet browser wallet version 2.68.0 was injected with a malicious backdoor, affecting numerous users and causing losses exceeding **$7 million**.

### 3. AI Deepfake & Social Engineering: Systemic Collapse of Digital Identity Trust

The rapid development of AI has shifted social engineering attacks from static scams to **dynamic, personalized interactions**. In Web3 social engineering attacks, over **56%** involved AI deepfake technology, and losses due to AI deepfake combined with social engineering **grew by 3000%** in 2025.

**Mechanism:**

By leveraging large AI models, attackers can generate real-time **fake video/audio** and combine this with big data analysis of victims’ social footprints to conduct **spear-phishing attacks**. The core is simulating human interaction behavior: AI agents can engage in extended conversations, persuading users to install malware, share private keys, or authorize transactions.

**AI Deepfake Attacks Typically Follow Four Stages:**

1. **Intelligence Gathering:** Collect victim information from X, Telegram, Discord, and select targets.
2. **Fake Generation:** Use AI tools to create video/audio deepfakes.
3. **Interactive Inducement:** Build trust via video calls or voice chats, persuading users to install malware, share private keys, or perform transactions.
4. **Execution & Escape:** After asset transfer, traces are erased. Attack costs are significantly lower, and success rates increase by over 50%.

### 4. AI-Powered Smart Contract Vulnerability Discovery: The Arms Race of Automated Offense and Defense

In 2025, **AI-driven smart contract vulnerability discovery** demonstrated the ability to independently identify and exploit smart contract flaws, accelerating a fundamental shift in the traditional paradigm of contract auditing and exploitation.

**Mechanism:**

AI models such as **Claude Sonnet 4.5+** and **GPT-5+**, trained on massive volumes of Solidity code, simulate execution paths to identify syntax errors, logical flaws, and other smart contract vulnerabilities. These models can then automatically generate exploit scripts, enabling **fully automated vulnerability discovery and exploitation**.

**AI-Based Smart Contract Vulnerability Discovery Typically Involves Three Stages:**

1. **Vulnerability Scanning:** AI analyzes contract code to identify zero-day vulnerabilities.
2. **Simulated Validation:** AI evaluates and validates the exploitability of discovered vulnerabilities.
3. **Exploit Execution:** AI generates attack code to carry out automated exploitation.

**Further Cases:**

In 2025, long-running smart contracts such as **Balancer** and **Yearn**, despite years of secure operation, continued to have new vulnerabilities uncovered. This highlights the rapidly increasing capability of attackers to leverage AI for discovering **deep, non-obvious smart contract vulnerabilities**.

### Chapter 4: GoPlus Security Recommendations

GoPlus believes that the future of Web3 security is no longer just about **“catching bad actors” or “finding vulnerabilities”**, but about providing **ubiquitous, infrastructure-level (chain-native) and intelligent (AI-powered) protection** for users’ assets — protection that users barely notice, yet feel completely secure.

**For Users:**

- Always maintain the habit of **“Don’t click, don’t sign, don’t install, don’t transfer.”** Do not click unknown links, sign unclear transactions, install untrusted software, or transfer funds to unfamiliar addresses.
- Download wallet apps **only from official channels** and cross-verify via social media accounts, DefiLlama, CoinGecko, CMC, etc.
- Install security plugins and stay alert for phishing links claiming “airdrops” or “prizes.”
- Always **verify signature contents and transaction intent**; refuse blind signing.
- When purchasing tokens, verify the **token contract address** and check token risk.
- Do not store large amounts in hot wallets; buy cold wallets through official channels.
- Keep private keys/mnemonics secure: handwrite multiple copies, store offline, protect from fire, moisture, and carelessness, **never connect to the internet, screenshot, or upload to cloud**. Anyone asking for your private key/mnemonic is **100% a scammer**.

**For Projects and Chain Ecosystems:**

Projects and chains need to build a **system-level security framework** that goes beyond single-point security, covering the **entire lifecycle from development to user interaction.**

- **Development Stage — Security Left Shift:** Use secure contract templates to reduce vulnerabilities from the start; integrate AI auditing tools for continuous, automated vulnerability screening before deployment, addressing threats from AI-driven exploit discovery.
- **Operation Stage — Full-Cycle Monitoring:** Integrate security APIs to provide users with token detection, malicious address databases, authorization risk assessment, and transaction simulation; implement transparent lockups using multisig + asset locking tools to demonstrate liquidity security and increase trust.
- **Response Stage — Rapid Emergency Mechanism:** Collaborate with security teams and pre-establish communication with security service providers to respond quickly to incidents, minimizing losses.
- **Native Security:** Upgrade security from the **“application layer”** to the **“protocol and base layers”**, creating proactive security capabilities.

**Security is the essential path for Web3 to achieve mass adoption. 2025 security data and technological trends indicate that security is no longer an after-the-fact cost — it is a fundamental infrastructure that determines whether Web3 can go mainstream.**
