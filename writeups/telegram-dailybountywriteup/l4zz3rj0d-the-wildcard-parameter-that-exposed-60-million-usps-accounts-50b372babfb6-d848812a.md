Third one in the API rotation, and this is the one worth teaching to anyone who thinks authorization bugs only happen at flashy startups with sloppy engineering. This hit the United States Postal Service, found by a researcher who chose to stay anonymous, reported directly to USPS more than a year before anything happened, and completely ignored the entire time until Brian Krebs got involved and gave USPS forty-eight hours of public pressure to actually fix it. [Krebs’s original reporting](https://krebsonsecurity.com/2018/11/usps-site-exposed-data-on-60-million-users/) is the primary account here, worth reading in full. Real respect owed to a researcher who sat on a genuinely severe finding responsibly for over a year, through total silence from the vendor, rather than just dropping it publicly out of frustration.

USPS runs a program called Informed Visibility, built for businesses and bulk mail senders who want near real-time tracking data on their mail campaigns, how many pieces are moving, where they are, when they’ll land. Behind that feature sits an API, and like most APIs built for logged-in account holders to query their own data, it needed some way to accept search terms, an email address, an account number, a tracking reference, and return whatever records matched.

The actual flaw was almost disarmingly simple to state, which is exactly why it’s worth sitting with rather than skipping past as too basic to matter: the API accepted wildcard search parameters, meaning you could submit a search that matched not just one specific record, but every record in the entire data set, without needing to know or guess a single real username, email, or account number belonging to anyone else. Any logged-in usps.com user, with nothing more than a basic understanding of how to open their browser’s developer console and edit a request parameter, could turn a “search for my own account” feature into “return every account on the platform.”

What makes this worth actually studying rather than just filing under “yet another exposed API” is the specific shape of the failure, because it’s subtly different from the IDOR pattern covered earlier in this series on [Facebook](https://medium.com/@l4zz3rj0d/how-four-lines-of-code-could-delete-any-facebook-photo-album-68626d5766b6) and [Meta AI](https://medium.com/@l4zz3rj0d/how-a-guessable-number-exposed-every-meta-ai-users-private-prompts-8b3ec5e707af). An IDOR is usually about swapping one specific ID for another specific ID you’re not supposed to have, a targeted substitution. This was broader and in some ways lazier than that, the application never actually implemented the boundary at all, it wasn’t that the check could be bypassed with a clever trick, there was functionally no check verifying that the account making a request was only allowed to see data belonging to itself. Nicholas Weaver, a researcher at UC Berkeley’s International Computer Science Institute, put it about as plainly as this kind of bug can be put when Krebs reached out to him for comment: this wasn’t a subtle lapse in a sophisticated access control system, it was the complete absence of the single most basic rule any system handling other people’s data has to enforce, that a request for data has to be checked against who’s actually asking for it.

The practical result was full query access across roughly 60 million accounts, email addresses, usernames, user IDs, account numbers, street addresses, phone numbers, and in some cases, data tied to other authorized users on the same account. Multiple accounts sharing a single data point, a street address, say, could all surface together off a single search, meaning the blast radius of even a single, lazy query extended well past whatever the attacker specifically went looking for. And the API didn’t stop at reading. Krebs’s own limited testing found that the same promiscuous access extended to writing, requesting changes to another user’s account details, email, phone number, the works, though USPS had at least put a confirmation-email step in front of some of those specific changes, which is the one narrow place this story has a silver lining buried in it.

The part of this story worth sitting with longest though isn’t the technical mechanism at all, it’s the year of silence. The researcher did everything right, found a severe, easily-demonstrated authorization failure on a federal agency’s systems, and reported it responsibly rather than exploiting it or dumping it. USPS simply never responded. That gap, more than a year, is worth remembering as its own lesson for anyone doing this work seriously: responsible disclosure is a two-way commitment, and when the receiving side doesn’t hold up their end, the researcher’s actual leverage very often isn’t more technical proof, it’s bringing in someone whose job is making silence costly. Krebs reaching out directly is what actually got USPS to patch this within 48 hours, the exact same pattern that played out with [Peloton](https://medium.com/@l4zz3rj0d/how-peloton-confused-authentication-with-authorization-714b8b70116e) and Pen Test Partners being ignored until TechCrunch got involved, a pattern that shows up often enough across this entire series that it’s worth naming as its own recurring truth about this work: finding the bug and getting it fixed are sometimes two completely separate problems requiring two completely different skills.

The generalizable test worth carrying out of this one is almost embarrassingly simple to actually run, and that’s exactly why it’s worth running on every single API endpoint you ever test that accepts a search or filter parameter: try submitting a wildcard, an empty string, a single percent sign, an asterisk, anything that means “match everything” rather than “match this one specific thing,” and watch whether the server actually enforces that your results stay scoped to data you’re authorized to see, or whether it just runs whatever query you handed it against the entire table. A search feature that works perfectly when you query your own data tells you nothing about whether it’s actually checking authorization at all, it might just be returning the one record that happens to match your legitimate, narrow search by coincidence. The only way to know for certain is to ask it, deliberately, for everything at once.
