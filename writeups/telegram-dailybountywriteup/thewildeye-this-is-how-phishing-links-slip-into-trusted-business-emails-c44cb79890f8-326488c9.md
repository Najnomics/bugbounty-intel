*How a vulnerable purchase-inquiry workflow allowed attacker-controlled links to reach recipients through legitimate business emails.*

**By Vyom Nagpal | Cybersecurity Researcher**

*How a seemingly harmless business inquiry became a potential phishing delivery channel, exposing a weakness in the way applications handle user-generated content.*

Most phishing attacks try to earn your trust before you even click a link.

They arrive disguised as payment notifications, account alerts, delivery updates, or business requests. The attacker impersonates someone you know, hoping you will lower your guard.

But what happens when the phishing link arrives through a business platform you already trust?

That was the question I ended up investigating while examining a purchase-inquiry workflow during a security assessment.

The feature itself was straightforward. A buyer could submit a product requirement, enter a description, and send the request to a supplier. The platform would process the information and automatically generate an email notification for the recipient.

Nothing unusual about that.

However, something caught my attention while examining the description field. It accepted externally hosted URLs alongside ordinary product information.

Accepting a URL is not automatically a vulnerability. Businesses regularly share catalogues, product specifications, and reference websites.

What mattered was what happened to that URL after the form was submitted.

I decided to follow the data through the application rather than stopping at the input field.

For testing, I submitted an ordinary purchase inquiry containing a product description and an external URL.

The application accepted the request and processed it through its usual workflow.

I then examined the email generated after submission.

The platform had included the submitted description in its notification to the recipient. The external URL was there too.

The email looked like a completely normal business communication. It informed the recipient that a buyer had submitted a purchase request, displayed the requirement details, and asked them to review the request and respond.

But the link inside the email was not controlled by the platform.

It had originated from the user-submitted description.

This was the point where the finding became more interesting.

The issue was no longer limited to accepting an external URL inside a form. The application was taking attacker-controlled content and delivering it through an email generated by a legitimate business workflow.

Think about how most people handle business emails. A notification from a platform they regularly use is naturally more convincing than an unsolicited message from an unfamiliar sender.

An attacker could potentially take advantage of that existing trust to distribute links to fraudulent websites.

The application was effectively lending credibility to content it did not control.

I wanted to understand the potential consequences of someone interacting with the external destination.

I continued testing in a controlled environment and monitored the resulting activity.

The terminal output recorded multiple link-open events and indicated that network information and location data had been received by the testing environment.

A separate console capture contained further activity, including repeated target-open events and messages indicating that camera-related files had been received.

The original evidence also contained location-related output, along with a corresponding map view.

These results helped demonstrate why the delivery of an attacker-controlled URL through a trusted notification channel deserves attention.

Depending on the external destination and the recipient’s interaction, a malicious page could attempt to collect visitor information, redirect users to fraudulent login pages, or encourage further actions through social engineering.

The distinction between the application and the external destination is important. The application enabled the delivery of the URL; the subsequent collection activity was performed by the external testing destination.

The original testing material went beyond terminal logs. It also included a sequence of captured images, individual image-viewer evidence, and additional capture output.

Another portion of the console output contained fields labelled with IP, account, and login information.

I have intentionally removed personal information, facial details, account identifiers, credential-like values, network identifiers, precise location data, and live destinations from the publication material.

These screenshots provide supporting context for the controlled testing activity. They should not be interpreted as proof that the original application bypassed browser camera permissions or that a real supplier account was compromised.

The actual application-level finding remains the propagation of user-controlled external links into a trusted, platform-generated business notification.

That is the behaviour that needs to be addressed.

From an application-security perspective, the issue lies in how user-controlled content travels through the purchase-inquiry workflow.

The description field accepts user input. That information is then processed by the application and included in a generated email.

The important security boundary is between untrusted input and trusted system-generated content.

An application cannot assume that information is safe simply because it has been submitted through a legitimate form. The same content may eventually appear in HTML pages, email templates, notifications, or other components.

Each of those output contexts needs the appropriate security controls.

In this case, an externally supplied URL could travel from the original form into a notification that carried the credibility of the platform itself.

I would classify the behaviour as **stored phishing injection through user-controlled purchase-inquiry content**, resulting in trusted-channel phishing.

It is worth distinguishing this from Cross-Site Scripting (XSS). The available evidence demonstrates that an external link was propagated into a generated email. It does not establish arbitrary JavaScript execution within the application.

The relevant weakness is primarily insufficient control over user-supplied content and its subsequent presentation.

The potential impact goes beyond a recipient opening an unfamiliar website.

Imagine an attacker submitting a purchase request containing a link to a fraudulent supplier portal.

A recipient receives the notification through a familiar business workflow. The message contains an apparently legitimate purchase requirement, and the external destination looks relevant to the request.

Depending on the page and the recipient’s interaction, this could lead to credential-harvesting attempts or exposure of visitor information.

The same approach could support supplier impersonation, fraudulent business communications, or further attempts to compromise partner accounts.

There is also a reputational dimension. Even when the malicious website is hosted externally, recipients may associate the phishing email with the platform that delivered it.

This is what makes trusted-channel phishing particularly concerning. The attacker is not necessarily creating trust from scratch. They are attempting to exploit trust that already exists.

Blocking the exact destination observed during testing would be an incomplete solution.

Attackers can register new domains, use URL shorteners, introduce redirect chains, or move malicious content to different hosting infrastructure.

The more important fix is to secure the complete path taken by user-submitted content.

The application should treat purchase descriptions as untrusted input and validate them on the server. Where rich formatting is not necessary, plain-text handling is preferable. If HTML formatting is supported, a strict allow-list sanitizer should be used.

Output encoding is equally important. User-controlled content must be escaped according to the context in which it is rendered, particularly when inserted into HTML email templates. Input validation alone does not guarantee safe output.

External URLs should be parsed and normalized before being accepted. Unsupported schemes, malformed destinations, and suspicious URL structures should be rejected. Reputation checks and redirect inspection can provide additional protection.

The generated email should also clearly distinguish externally supplied links from platform-owned links. Recipients should be warned when a destination is external rather than being encouraged to treat every link inside the notification as trusted.

Finally, monitoring should cover suspicious inquiry submissions, repeated URL abuse, and unusual sender activity. Regression testing should verify that user-controlled content cannot alter email templates, introduce active HTML, or disguise external destinations.

The objective is not to prevent businesses from sharing legitimate links. It is to ensure that a legitimate application does not unintentionally become a phishing delivery mechanism.

The finding was documented as a high-severity vulnerability involving user-controlled purchase-inquiry content and its propagation into generated business emails.

The original assessment recorded a proposed CVSS v3.1 score of 8.1, with CWE-20 (Improper Input Validation).

The affected company and product identifiers are intentionally omitted from this publication. The vulnerability description, observed behaviour, and supporting evidence are retained for the responsible disclosure and CVE-reference process.

What made this finding interesting was not the complexity of the testing.

It was how ordinary the entire workflow appeared.

A buyer submitted a request. The platform processed it. An email was generated. A supplier received it.

Every step looked like a normal business operation.

Yet, somewhere between the original form submission and the final notification, the distinction between trusted platform content and untrusted user input was not adequately enforced.

This is why application-security assessments should follow data beyond the point where it enters the application.

A text field might appear harmless on its own. Its security implications change when that same content is stored, processed, and delivered to another person using the platform’s own communication infrastructure.

Sometimes, the biggest weakness isn’t an exposed database or a complicated authentication bypass.

Sometimes, it is a completely ordinary form that allows the wrong content to travel to the right person.

**The email was legitimate. The link inside it had no reason to be trusted.**
