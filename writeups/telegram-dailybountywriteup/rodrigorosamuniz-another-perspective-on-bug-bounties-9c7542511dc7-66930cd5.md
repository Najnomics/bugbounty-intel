I read a lot of articles about bug bounties. At least in Brazil, most focus on techniques, bypasses, or how rewarding it can be to earn extra income or make a living from bug hunting. And those articles really are informative and useful.

I wanted to write for security professionals in Brazil, but take a different angle: managing a bug bounty program and how it can strengthen and scale security within a company.

I’m not a bug hunter myself, although plenty of friends have invited me to give it a try. With a busy schedule, I have to choose how I spend my time, and these days I prioritize my family and hobbies. Yes, getting older changes your priorities. Maybe one day I’ll get my hands dirty and shake the rust off my technical skills?

Okay, now that I’ve made these disclaimers, I’ll try to lay out my thoughts on the subject.

I’ve worked at a few companies where I’ve seen a Bug Bounty Program (or BBP) implemented. On those occasions, I was an observer, but at one of them I led the implementation from start to finish and was responsible for maintaining the program. When I say “observer,” I’m referring to my role as a technical security lead, overseeing the Bug Bounty Program from an internal perspective. In that role, I was responsible for communicating any findings or critical vulnerabilities to the technical development teams, as well as directly managing the triage and lifecycle of vulnerabilities identified through the program.

Before taking on the direct responsibility of implementing a bug bounty program from scratch, I often found myself plagued by intrusive thoughts about the risks of failure. I worried about the possibility of poor implementation, a lack of proper prioritization of vulnerabilities by internal teams, the resulting disengagement of researchers, and, as a result, a negative impact on the reputation of the company and the security professionals involved. These risks can, in fact, materialize if the program’s purpose is not clear to the organization. However, today, after having participated in several programs in various roles, I realize that the chances of success for a well-structured bug bounty program far outweigh the risks of failure. With proper planning, the opportunities are exponentially greater than the challenges in any company that is even minimally concerned with information security issues.

I say this because a bug bounty program takes your company’s security to a level that no internal security team could ever achieve. It allows ethical researchers to exploit gaps and vulnerabilities in the darkest corners of your company, including legacy systems or shadow IT areas that have been forgotten and left unmaintained. This is wonderful because it provides a holistic view of your environment, assessed by highly qualified professionals who are well-ranked on bug bounty platforms.

Furthermore, a well-executed, thoroughly evaluated, and clearly explained proof of concept regarding the risk of exploiting a specific vulnerability has the power to engage any development team. It conveys the urgency needed to resolve the problem, fostering collaboration and engagement among everyone involved in the process.

These facts are undeniable. The program is paying for itself and demonstrating its value in a pragmatic way.

Now, to reach that level of certainty that everything will go well and the program will be a success, I believe it’s worth highlighting a few points that, in my view, make all the difference:

It will always be very rewarding to have impressive numbers to use as indicators of your program’s success, such as: resolving thousands of vulnerabilities in a year or having initiatives like a Researcher Hall of Fame, which demonstrate researchers’ engagement with your program and help increase the resilience and security of an ecosystem. But keep in mind that these aren’t the only indicators of success for a good bug bounty program.

Having a clear understanding of the expected outcomes of the bug bounty program, knowing how to reward and interact appropriately with researchers and the security community, providing appropriate rewards, and addressing vulnerabilities found through the program in a timely manner are also excellent indicators of your program’s success.
