Back to AI for this round, and this one’s barely two months old at time of writing, which is rare for this series but worth making an exception for. Found by Harsh Jaiswal, Mohan Pedhapati, and Rahul Maini at Hacktron AI in July 2026, reported responsibly, fixed in roughly 14 hours, and paid a $6,500 bounty. Full technical writeup straight from the team: [Hacking OpenAI](https://www.hacktron.ai/blog/hacking-openai). Genuine respect due here on more than one front, they stopped the moment they’d proven impact rather than reading a single line of OpenAI’s actual source code, and they were upfront that OpenAI explicitly excluded part of what they’d done from the bounty’s scope, and they said so plainly rather than inflating the story.

Worth being straight about something before getting into the mechanics: this report leans on a genuinely serious memory-corruption exploit chain, a heap buffer overflow in an image-decoding library most people have never heard of, discovered and weaponized largely by directing Claude through the process rather than hand-writing the exploit themselves. That part’s a fascinating story on its own about where AI-assisted security research actually stands right now. But it’s not the part OpenAI’s own bounty payout was actually for, and it’s not the part worth building this piece around, since this series is specifically about the bugs that take real human reasoning to even think to look for. The access-control flaw sitting underneath the exploit chain, the thing OpenAI actually paid $6,500 to fix, is a textbook case of exactly that.

The Hacktron team’s starting point wasn’t a scanner pointed at OpenAI’s infrastructure, it was a hypothesis formed purely from understanding how OpenAI’s own services fit together. OpenAI runs its public community forum, community.openai.com, on a product called Discourse, and lets people log into that forum using "Sign in with OpenAI" through a shared identity system. The team's actual insight, before touching a single exploit, was recognizing that a shared login system connecting a low-stakes public forum to high-stakes internal tools like ChatGPT and Codex is exactly the kind of boundary worth testing, because shared identity infrastructure only works safely if every service on the other end of it actually checks not just that a token is valid, but that it's valid for the specific thing being asked of it.

That hypothesis needed proof, and proof meant actually landing inside the forum with a compromised session first, which is where the memory-corruption side of the story comes in only as a means to an end. Discourse was routing a specific image format through a chain ending in a library called libheif, and a security fix that had quietly gone out upstream, without being flagged as a security fix and without a CVE attached, had never made it into the Debian packages Discourse's own Docker image was built on. That's a detail worth sitting with on its own: a fix existing somewhere upstream doesn't help you if the specific build you're running never actually received it, and nobody downstream had reason to go check, because nothing about the original commit advertised itself as something worth backporting urgently.

With working code execution on the forum in hand, the actual test of the original hypothesis was almost anticlimactic in how directly it confirmed what they’d suspected: a session token issued by that forum, meant for nothing more than reading and posting on a help board, was also being honored by ChatGPT and Codex as a fully valid login. No re-check at the boundary of either of those higher-trust applications asking “wait, was this token actually meant for us, or just for the forum that issued it.” Log into the forum, and depending on which OpenAI employees happened to have logged in through that same path, you could step straight into their live ChatGPT and Codex sessions with no further interaction needed from them at all.

To actually demonstrate the real-world severity of that without crossing a line into reading anything they shouldn’t, the team took over one employee’s account whose Codex environment happened to be connected to OpenAI’s own internal GitHub organization, and had that compromised Codex session open a single, deliberately harmless pull request inside OpenAI’s internal monorepo, nothing more than a renamed README file, proof the access chain reached all the way to production source control without ever actually touching proprietary code. Then they stopped, reported, and waited.

This is really the core lesson worth carrying out of the whole story, independent of how impressive the memory-corruption half of it reads: the root failure here was never really about the forum, or the image library, or even AI-assisted exploit development specifically. It was a single sentence worth of missing logic, somewhere in OpenAI’s identity infrastructure, that should have asked “is this token scoped for the service that’s receiving it” and never did. Shared single sign-on is supposed to make managing identity across many services easier and more consistent, but every single service trusting that same identity provider has to independently enforce that a token issued for purpose A doesn’t quietly unlock purpose B just because both purposes happen to trust the same login screen. That’s not a bug you find by fuzzing an endpoint, it’s a bug you find by actually mapping out how a company’s services connect to each other and asking, deliberately, whether the boundaries between “low stakes” and “high stakes” are actually as separate as the org chart implies.

Worth carrying forward into your own work on exactly this basis: any time you’re testing a target that has more than one surface sharing a login system, a forum and a main app, a support portal and a billing dashboard, a marketing site and an admin panel, the one question worth asking by hand before touching any exploit tooling at all is whether a token minted on the “unimportant” side is actually checked for scope before the “important” side accepts it, or whether every surface is just trusting the same signature and calling it a day.
